In June of 1975, Warren and Nehemiah Bunn Doubleday sat down and interviewed himself and recorded two segments about his boyhood life in the Swift River Valley. The first side of this copy deals with his grandfather's sawmill at the area known as Doubleday Village in North Dana. The second side deals with the general store, which was run by his father, Myron Doubleday, also in North Dana, and of Bunn's experiences as a boy living and working in the store. This is now copied in July of 1988 by Audrey Dukert. This is what I remember about my grandfather Doubleday's sawmill in North Dana. It was part of the place which he bought in 1883, the year my father, Myron Doubleday, was born. And when my grandfather, Rowland Doubleday, bought it, it was known as the Gleason Place. And it included not only the farm and pastures, but the mill site as well. The mill was a water-powered mill located on Fever Brook in North Dana. And the dam was just below the...where the two branches, east and west branches of Fever Brook came together. The road from North Dana to Dana Center went across part of the mill pond on a bridge. The dam being a couple hundred yards below this road on the book. As I recall it, the dam was about ten feet high, and it was built of stone. At one end of the dam was the control works, and from this a wooden pinstock ran down to the mill. This pinstock, as I recall, was square, made of four large planks, each about two feet wide, making the pinstock about two feet square, and held together by iron straps and iron bars. This pinstock led down to the water wheel underneath the mill. This was not the old type of overshot wheel or undershot wheel, but it was a turbine type, which probably at that time was quite modern. This turbine was inside of a steel case, which I remember as being about four feet in diameter and perhaps four feet tall. The pinstock could be opened and closed by a wheel up on the floor of the mill, and the ladder turbine or ladder wheel drove the main shaft, which in turn drove a leather belt, which I remember as being over a foot wide, perhaps fifteen inches wide, and this led up to the shaft of the saw. Of course, it also ran any other machinery that was in the mill, too. The saw was probably about four feet in diameter, so it was capable of handling large what to two feet in diameter. The mill was normally operated by three men, a roller, a sawyer, and a marker, and if the force was available, he handled the slabs. The roller took the lads which were on the skidway and rolled them from there onto the carriage. Now, the carriage ran on a set of rails, similar to railroad rails, only smaller, and the carriage was probably around eighteen or twenty feet long altogether, and was made so that the upper part of the carriage could be moved crossways of the carriage toward the saw. The carriage ran back and forth right past the saw. So the roller rolled a log onto the carriage, and it was held there by two heavy iron dogs, which were sharp-pointed and were raised up and down on steel posts. The roller would jam these two dogs into the log, and this would hold it. The sawyer would then move the carriage over by means of a handle, which he could pull on, move the carriage over, so that as it passed by the saw, the saw would cut off a section of the log. And the first section that was cut off was called the slab, because this contained mostly bark. And usually the way they did it was they would saw off the slab, in other words, take the first cut. Then the sawyer would run the carriage backwards, and then move the carriage over a certain amount. If he wanted a one-inch board, he would move it one inch. If he wanted a two-inch board, he would move it two inches, and so on. And then he would run the carriage forward past the saw again, and the saw would cut off whatever thickness of board the sawyer had moved the carriage for. After they'd taken off several boards this way, they would then roll the log over, so that the part which had already been sawed would be flat up against the carriage. And this time the two dogs were turned around, so that a shorter dog would be holding the log in place on the carriage, so that the saw could saw up fairly close to the end. Then they would continue the process of running the carriage forward past the saw. The first cut would take off a slab, and then the next cut would take off boards, which had round edges. In other words, they still had the bark on the two sides of them. Now, if they wanted to have square-edge boards, they would merely cut four slabs off of the log, leaving a four-sided log, which had no bark left on it at all. And they would then proceed as before, and this would result in square-edge boards. Now, as the carriage went past the saw, the boards, which had been sawed off from the log, would just fall over onto a bench. And then the marker would mark on it the number of boards feet in that board. Of course, a board foot is a board one foot wide, one foot long, and one inch thick. So if you had a board which was ten feet long, one foot wide, and one inch thick, that board would have ten board feet in it. And the marker would add a piece of heavy crayon, and he would mark a ten on that board. If the board was the same size but two inches thick, it would have twenty board feet in it, and he'd mark a twenty on it, and so on. He also had a peg board hanging on the wall, and by moving the pegs into different holes in this board, he was able to keep track of the number of board feet of lumber that was sawed during the day by the mill. And on a good day, they would saw several thousand board feet of lumber. A teamster would then take this lumber, which had just been sawed, and load it onto his wagon, or, if it was in the wintertime, his sled. And with a pair of horses, he would draw it from the mill out to the area where it was stuck up, so to speak. Sticking up this lumber consisted of first laying down some heavy pieces of slab on the ground, so as to get a fairly level foundation to start with, and also get it up off of the ground, because if the lumber was piled directly on the ground, it would rot very quickly. He would lay a couple of these heavy pieces of slab crossways, and then start laying the planks on top of them. And he would strap with a pile, perhaps five or six feet wide. When he got one layer of planks or boards laid down, he would then lay a couple of board sticks, crossways of this layer of boards, and then put another layer on top. These bullet sticks were made by taking slabs in the mill and sawing them first into the proper length, five, six feet, whatever size they wanted for the lumber piles, and then splitting the slabs by means of a saw into pieces, which would then be five feet long and about one inch thick. They would vary in width, but this didn't make any difference, of course. And these board sticks were what separated the layers of boards or planks in the piles of lumber. And this allowed air to circulate through the piles of lumber, and therefore cure or dry the wood in the lumber. And it was ordinarily cured for about a year before it was sold. Then, if it was sold, it might have been sold to the local box factory, the Swiss Triller Box Company in North Dana, for making wooden boxes, in which case it would be drawn by a team of horses from the yard, so to speak, where the piles of lumber were, down to the box shop. Or if it was sold to some out of town concern, my grandfather and my father sold quite a lot of lumber to the furniture factories over around Gardner and Templeton. If it was sold to one of them, it would be drawn down to North Dana and loaded into railroad box cars, and then shipped by railroad over to the furniture factory. By the time I was big enough to see the mill, my grandfather had stopped operating it, and instead it was operated by Frank Doubleday. But I used to go over there as a kid quite often, because it was only a quarter of a mile or so from my home there in North Dana. And particularly, one period when my uncle, Glenn Page, worked there for Frank Doubleday, and Glenn was the roller. So I used to go over and watch him, and he would instruct me in the proper techniques of rolling, and so on and so forth, and I would also watch the operation of the mill. Another thing, too, I used to go over there quite often in the springtime, spearing fish, because after the mill had operated all day, and then they shut down around 5 o'clock in the afternoon, and then they shut down the mill. The perch in particular, yellow perch, and suckers would have come up through the brook during the daytime, and when they shut down the mill, of course, the water in the brook below the mill would suddenly lower by quite a good amount. So then you could see the fish in the brook, and with a pound spear on the end of a pole, we could spear them, and take them home to eat. Or as frequently happened, it was too much work to dress them, so I'd take them home and give them to the cat. Now this mill, of course, being powered by water from the pond and the dam, could not be moved like the steam mills of that day. Those mills were portable mills. They could be moved around from one lot to another, and even could be set up several different times on the same lot if it was a big one. But this mill was permanently located, so all the labs had to be drawn in there to be sawed. And, of course, this meant that it handled mostly only timber from right around the immediate vicinity, because it was not economical to draw it from too great a distance. The sardust that was made by the saw cutting off the boards from the logs, the sardust was blown out through a pipe, through a pile quite near the mill. And, of course, over the course of years, why, this pile of sardust got pretty high, and it was a lot of fun to climb up and down this pile of sardust. Also, the sardust was used, for example, it was used for bedding for horses and cattle, but it was also used in storing ice. Ice would be cut in a pond in the wintertime, and it was stored for summer use by being covered around the side and top with sawdust from the mill. So that was one use that they had for the sawdust. The slabs also, over a period of years, collected into some pretty big piles. At the mill, the slabs were sawed up into lengths of four or five feet, piled onto a little truck which also operated on small rails similar to railroad rails. And when the truck was filled with slabs, it would be pushed out by hand, out to an area away from the mill, and then the slabs piled up into piles there. And some people used these slabs for firewood, but since they were a good percentage of them was just bark, there wasn't a great deal of heat in them in the firewood, so if you could afford it, you bought better firewood than what the slabs would provide. But, of course, pine slabs, slabs of pine wood or hemlock, made good kindling for your wood fire anyway. So a lot of people used slabs for that purpose, even though they would use hardwood for actual firewood. I should have said that the rower, in rolling the logs from the skidway onto the carriage, didn't just roll these logs by hand, but he used either a cant hook or a peavey to assist him in rolling the logs. They can't hook or the peavey, as far as I know, they were the same, there was no difference between them. They consisted of a heavy wooden handle, perhaps four feet long, this varied, of course, in individual cases. But, on one end was a steel point with a movable bog on the end of an arm, and when you put this cant hook against the log, the bog was pointed sharp and arranged at a proper angle, so that if you went to turn the log, the bog would bite into the log, and you had this four or five foot handle, which gave you the leverage, and a man that way could roll a pretty big log, which was much bigger than one that he could just handle by hand, without any cant hook or peavey. I mentioned the firewood, and my home there in North Boehner, when I was small, was heated entirely by wood. We had about an eight-room house, and we had a cast-iron wood stove in the kitchen, in which my mother did all the cooking, of course, and the rest of the house was heated by a furnace. That furnace was located in the cellar, and it consisted of an iron firebox, about three feet wide and six feet long. It would handle, would take six foot long wood, and then around this was a brick structure, and from the interior of this brick section, sheet metal pipes ran to the different rooms of the house, and conducted the hot air from the furnace up to the different rooms. The hot air pipes were similar to those in use today, and at the end of them in the room was a register, which could be opened and closed on the floor to provide more or less heat. As I said, the furnace was capable of handling wood up to six feet long, but we never used anything but four foot wood in it, simply because it was more of a standard length than was the normal way to cut wood. My father would hire some local woodchopper to go into the woods, on land that my dad owned, and cut firewood. And the woodchopper would chop down the trees with an axe, and then chop them up with an axe into four foot lengths. If the tree was large enough so that a four foot length of it would be pretty heavy for one man to handle, in other words, if it weighed more than, say, a hundred pounds, where he would then split it into smaller pieces by means of steel wedges and sledgehammer. But we would get pieces of wood four feet long and up to, or perhaps, twelve inches in diameter. Dad would then hire some local teamster to draw this wood from the woods down to the house, with a pair of horses and teams. He would pile it up outside the house, just above the bulkhead, which opened into the cellar, and we would use about five quads of wood every winter in the furnace to heat the house. And my job was to keep the wood in the cellar where Dad could put it into the furnace. I'd have to go up to the top of the bank there where it was piled up and throw it from there down into the open bulkhead of the cellar. And, of course, if you weren't careful, instead of the stick of wood landing in the open bulkhead, it might hit the side of the house. But with a little practice, you could get them to go pretty close to where you wanted them. Then we would pile up this wood down in the cellar more frequently, just leave it loosely as it landed from having thrown it in, and Dad would put it from there into the furnace. Of course, this wood was not green wood. It had to be cured. And usually it cured for one season. It would be cut one winter for use the following winter. And we used all kinds of wood, just as it came as it grew in the woods. In the woods, it would be mostly maple, probably, but quite a lot of oak. And some burricks, some papilla. Of course, burricks and papilla were not as good wood for heat as the hardwoods. There would also be some beech. Of course, in the early days, there would be chestnut. But by the time I was ten years old or so, most of the chestnut trees had died from the blight. So we didn't get much chestnut that I remember of to burn. And of course, we didn't care for the soft wood like pine and hemlock because there was not as much heat in those. Also, for the kitchen, we would use, as I remember, about two cords of wood a year. And so the teamster who brought the wood for the furnace would also leave about a couple of cords of it out by the woodshed. And then Dad would usually hire some local man either to saw it up by hand. Eddie Smith used to saw it by hand with a buck saw on a saw horse. Or sometimes Dad would hire some local man who owned a saw rig, as it was called, a gasoline-driven engine which ran a saw. And usually two men would operate this. One would take the four-foot sticks of wood and put them on the little carriage. And this carriage would tilt up against the saw. And he would saw a stick of wood. The four-foot wood would make three sticks of firewood as a rule. That would make each of them 16 inches long, of course. They would take these pieces of wood that had been cut from the four-foot wood and thrown into a pile. And then Dad or I or both of us would split that with an axe into pieces small enough to be easily used in the kitchen stove. And my job was to keep the wood box and the kitchen stove. We had a pretty big wood box. In fact, it was built in, in the partition between the kitchen and the back room, as we called it, so that the wood box could be filled from the back room without actually bringing the wood into the kitchen. But then there was a cover in the kitchen and you could reach in and get the wood from right in the back of the kitchen stove. And usually I would bring the wood in and just dump it into this big wood box, helter-skelder. But once in a while I'd get real ambitious and I would pack it in there very carefully. And then I wouldn't have to bring any more for several days. But most of the time I'd just bring in enough to go one day and then the following day I'd have to bring in some more. And I remember that my mother would sometimes have to speak to me kind of sharp to get me to go out and bring in some wood to fill the wood box with. Of course, when mother was baking anyway, she would usually use some good, fairly good sized oak wood, because that would burn longer. You wouldn't have to replenish the fire so often. But if she wanted a quick fire, well then she would find some pieces of birch or perhaps an occasional piece of pine or something like that. The softer wood, they would flare up and give her a hot fire more quickly. Later on, we put in a kerosene burner into that stove and this took the place of the wood. I was glad of that because it was much easier to fill the glass bottle with kerosene and bring that in than it was to bring in several baskets of wood. And one bottle of kerosene would probably run for about twenty-four hours. It was a pretty good sized bottle. About, I believe they held five gallons, although I'm not sure. It may have been a little smaller than that. But I liked that. But my mother didn't like it because it didn't give as good a fire. She didn't have as much control over it as she did over the wood fire. And she always claimed that she couldn't bake properly with that kerosene burner in the stove instead of the wood. So, in those days, of course, lots of other people were replacing their wood stoves with these kerosene heaters, converting them, I should say. And so there were all kinds of ideas for making the oven bake better. I know one thing was that we covered the top of the oven with brick. And this was supposed to somehow make the oven work better, but Mother said she couldn't see it had made any difference. And so she used this as an argument so that about as soon as electric stoves became available, why, she prevailed on Dad to get her an electric stove. And certainly that did work a lot better than the kerosene burner had worked in the old wood stove. I mentioned the four-foot wood that we used in the furnace. And Dad would light the fire in the furnace in the fall, and then that fire would never go out from that time until warm weather came the next spring. He would fix the fire in the morning at breakfast time, and then he would come home to lunch from the store, grocery store, and would fix it again at lunch time, and perhaps again at supper time. And then at bedtime, he would go down and load it up for the night. And he would throw in one or two big sticks, about as big as he could lift, oak if it was available, four feet long, and maybe up to a foot in diameter. And this would burn all night, and then the next morning there would be a good bed of cold, and all you would have to do would be to throw on some more wood, and it would all right to loom again. In that way, as I say, the fire never went out from fall until spring. And we never heated the bedrooms on the second floor of the house. They had hot air pipes leading to them, but we never heated them. We always slept in cold bedrooms. But the downstairs rooms, the dining room, the living room, and the parlor were all heated from the furnace. And we never had any trouble keeping warm, even in the coldest days, regardless of how windy it was or anything else. We were always perfectly comfortable in there. This is the first collection of North Dana. I pretty closely tied in with my father's grocery store. We always referred to it as a grocery store, although he sold quite a lot more than just gold. As I recall, the sign above the door read, of North Dana, I pretty closely tied in with my father's grocery store. We always referred to it as a grocery store, although he sold quite a lot more than just groceries. As I recall, the sign above the door read, M-N-Doubleday, dry goods, groceries, boots, shoes, and hardware. And he did sell all of those. In addition to the groceries, he, especially in the earlier days, he sold quite a bit of dry goods. For that I mean cloths for women to make dresses out of, house dresses, and stockings, and shoes, underwear. He sold quite a lot of shoes when I was small. Then, by, say, about 1920, many people had their own automobiles, so that they could drive to the larger towns like Assall or Orange, where they had a better stock to choose from. So, from that time on, he did not sell very many shoes. But when I was smaller, he sold shoes for men, women, and children. If I needed a pair of new shoes, I would go to the store, and Dad would sit me with a pair right there. I, in addition to that, he also sold, as the sign said, boots and hardware. Hardware included nails, hinges, door hardware, paint, kerosene, and turpentine, and shotgun shells, .22 rifle, bullets, and a few odds and ends. The groceries that he sold, of course, included sugar, flour, tea and coffee, and cereals, spices, extracts, and candy, and tobacco. The flour and sugar used to come to the store in barrels. And then we sold it by the pound, put it up in brown paper bags. Then, later on, of course, flour began to be packed in 25-pound bags. I don't recall if we ever had any smaller than that, put up by the flour companies, as they do today. But, in the early days, we sold a great deal of flour by the barrel. And, on some of the trips where we delivered groceries outside the village, why, it was not as all unusual to have two or three barrels of flour on the delivery wagon. Families would buy a barrel of flour in the flour, and that would carry them through the whole winter for their baking requirements. Of course, practically every woman baked her own bread in those days, as well as cakes, cookies, and pies, and so on. So we did quite a big business in bread flour. Later on, we sold baker's bread, so-called, and I remember that, at first, many women refused to buy it. They said it was no good. They could make a lot better bread themselves, and I agree with them. And then, gradually, however, they gave up the practice of baking their own bread to a great extent. And then the baker's bread came through already sliced. And, again, the women objected to this. They said the bread didn't keep as well when it was already sliced. And there was quite a bit of objection for quite a long while. We carried both sliced and unsliced bread. But, gradually, women accepted the sliced bread, and finally we stopped carrying unsliced bread completely. Sugar. Granulated sugar and brown sugar both came in barrels. These barrels were all of wood, the same as the flour barrels, but the sugar barrels were smaller than flour barrels, because, of course, sugar is heavier. As I recall, the flour barrels contained about a hundred pounds, and probably the sugar barrels did too. The granulated sugar we used to weigh out into one-pound lots, and two-pound lots, and five-pound lots, and ten-pound lots, each in a brown paper bag. And we would package the sugar during slack times during the store so that we'd have a supply of it, like, very handy, and wouldn't have to start to weigh it out whenever a lady wanted to buy a couple pounds of sugar. We used to deliver groceries around the village of North Dana three times a week, Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday. And on the alternate days, we delivered groceries outside the village. On Monday, we went on what we called the Doubleday Village trip. And on Wednesday, to Dana Center, and on Friday, to the edge of Greenwoods and Puska. The way we did this was, in the case of the days when we delivered around the village, we'd start out about half past seven in the morning, and the hired man, clerk, would visit one particular street, and write down the orders of the housewives in a little order book. And then he would come back to the store and leave that order book, and take another one, and go on a different street. And meanwhile, Dad would be putting up the orders that were taken from the first street. And after the clerk had been around all the streets, or for the streets, I should say, taking the orders, then, by that time, the first street would have the orders all ready for delivery. They would be packed in individual crates, as we called them. These were little wooden boxes with open spindles around the side, roughly two feet square, and maybe six or eight inches high. And each lady's order would be packed in a separate crate. So, when you were delivering groceries, like, you merely had to find some distinguishing article in each order, like, one order might have an order, contain ten pounds of sugar, but no flour, and so on. Another order might contain a pound of coffee, but no tea. And that way you could distinguish the order. You'd pick up that crate and carry it into the house, and leave it on the kitchen table. Then, for the outside trips, we would, the clerk would go around, for example, to the Doubleday Village area in the early morning, and come back. And as soon as he got back, well, he and Dad together would put up the orders that he had taken, and then, after dinner, after lunch, well, he would start out again and deliver those orders. So we did quite a brisk business in prohibition days of extract of vanilla and extract of ginger and things like that. We also sold veterinary medicines. We had a cabinet, I recall, of Dr. A. C. Daniel's veterinary medicines and we sold colic cure for horses and I don't recall many of the other specific things but farmers would occasionally come in and buy something like that for their farm animals. We did sell quite a lot of food that came packed in tin cans, especially vegetables like peas and beets and then fruit such as canned peaches and pears and things like that. All of this canned food came to us in wooden boxes. There were no cardboard boxes until nearly the time that Dad went out of the store business. But they, in fact, almost everything came packed in wooden boxes. And so we had a great many of these empty wooden boxes to get rid of. And when we emptied out the contents of the wooden box and put the cans under one of the shelves, then we would throw the empty box out the back door of the store. And then about once a year, we would take a day off when we could spare it and carry all these empty boxes and accumulated debris to the town dump. But these empty wooden boxes being out there right by the back door of the store meant that when we went home, such as to lunch or anything, we usually went out that back door. And in the summertime, I was nearly always barefoot. I hardly ever wore shoes in the summertime as a kid because I enjoyed the freedom of going barefoot. But two or three times during the summer, I would go out the back door and accidentally step on a nail, which was in the cover of one of these boxes, and stick this nail into my foot. So I would go back to the back door and say, Dad, I just stepped on a nail. He'd say, OK, come on. And he would go to the dry goods department where he kept the bolts of cloth. And he would take down a bolt of unbleached muslin and tear a strip off for a bandage. And then we would go back to the back end of the store where the hardware was kept. And he would wrap this bandage around my foot and then pour some serpentine onto the bandage. And it would sting for a minute. And he'd just say, OK, run along now. And so I'd go on about my business. And I never had any abscesses or inflammation from stuffing on one of those nails. I don't know whether the turpentine prevented it or whether I just was naturally immune. But I know that, as I say, I never had any trouble caused from stuffing on one of those nails anyway. When I was small, we did all the delivering of the groceries with a horse and wagon. Then later on we got an automobile truck. I would guess that we probably got the first one in about 1920 or somewhere along in there. But we always kept a horse or two at home to deliver the groceries with. And even after we got the auto truck for use, we still kept the horse because in the springtime it would be so muddy that particularly on the outside trips you couldn't use the truck anyway. You had to use a horse and wagon because of the mud. But we did have a Model T truck. And we used to use that not only to deliver the groceries but also to draw the freight, the crates of groceries and things from the railroad station up to the store. And when I was twelve years old, my dad began sending me all alone with the truck down to the railroad station to get the freight in spite of the fact that I was not able to have a driver's license until I was sixteen. This used to bother my mother quite a lot. She was afraid that somebody would report it. But as far as I know, no one ever did. At least nothing was ever done about it anyway. But occasionally, too, I would use the horse and get down with him to the railroad station to draw freight up to the store. Of course, I couldn't handle the big things like barrels of flour, but I could handle things like cases of canned goods and things like that without any trouble. When we had flour coming in, my dad or the clerk would go with me to bring that up from the railroad station. Of course, when I was quite smart, I really didn't enjoy working in the store very much, but as I got older, I got so that I tolerated it at least. Dad owned the store for one month short of twenty years, from 1909 to 1929. He had originally worked for Otis Hager in his store, and then he bought this other store in Mount Dana, which was, of course, in competition with Mr. Hager, and kept it, as I say, for practically twenty years. And as soon as he was out of the store business himself, the very next week he went right back to work at Hager's store, although by this time it was owned and operated by Lester Hager, Otis's son. Although they were competitors right there in the village, there were, at times, as many as four stores selling groceries in Mount Dana, and always there were at least two stores, Hager's and Doubleday's. So although we were competitors, we all still remained friends, and we frequently checked with each other to make sure that neither one was charging prices which were very much higher or very much lower than the other one. And also, if we had an order for a certain article, and we were temporarily out of it, we would stop over at Lester Hager's store and pick up the article, and he would do the same if he was out of something temporarily. In fact, the two families, my parents and Lester Hager and his wife, they used to socialize quite regularly outside of store hours. They and two other couples had a little bridge club in front of a bridge club, and they used to get together quite regularly to play bridge. And I recall one time that my father and mother and Lester and Mary and Hager took a vacation trip together. So the competition was always a friendly competition. In 1929, my father sold out to the Metropolitan District Water Supply Commission, and he sold all of his property in one lump sum. He owned a couple of houses and one or two timber lots and the store business, and this all went for one price. There was no way to distinguish how much you receive for any one of the units. But he always figured that either you could say that he got a low price for everything or that he got a good fair price for his real estate and nothing for his store business. But in any event, he probably did not receive anywhere near as much as he really should have and could have had he taken the matter to court. But he figured that in the long run he would be better off not going to court. But after he sold out to the state, we continued to live in the same house that we had been living in. He rented it from the state. And he and my mother and my sister lived there until 1938, that same place. And people frequently asked, well, what did you do for amusement there? Well, of course we did not have television in those days, but we did have radio after 1922 anyway. And there were such things as the Grange. Of course that meant twice a month. And nearly everyone in the village belonged to that. It was a regular social event, but then you also had church-sponsored events like parties and things of that sort. And then you had private parties, birthday parties and Halloween parties and things like that. And, of course, you could play cards. And in season we did a lot of hunting and fishing, swimming in the summertime. And my folks built a camp on Lake Nisiponset, which was about a mile from the village, so that we were able to just move up there and live there all summer long and operate the grocery store from there. Mother used to come down to the village on Mondays and do the family washing because we had the electric washing machine at home, of course. But I enjoyed those summers at camp, although, of course, we were pretty busy in the store, so we didn't have too much leisure time. But we had time to go fishing once in a while. And, as I said, we did a lot of hunting, too, around the area. My dad used to enjoy hunting partridges very much. We could take one or two afternoons a week off, or most of the afternoon, anyway, from the store in partridge season. We had a lot of different covers that we could go through one after another and drive from one to another in a car. And in that way, why, you could hunt all day long if you had the opportunity, because once you went through the list of covers that we had, where you could go back and start over again, and you could cover them maybe two or three times the same day and still see plenty of potters.
