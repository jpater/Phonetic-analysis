This is what I remember about my grandfather Doubleday's sawmill in North Dana. It was part of the place which he bought in 1883, the year my father, Myron Doubleday, was born. And when my grandfather, Myron Doubleday, bought it, it was known as the Gleason Place. And it included not only the farm and pastures but the mill site as well. The mill was a water-powered mill located on Fever Brook in North Dana. And the dam was just below the, where the two branches, east and west branches of Fever Brook, came together. The road from North Dana to Dana Center went across part of the mill pond on a bridge. The dam being a couple hundred yards below this road on the book. As I recall it, the dam was about ten feet high. And it was built of stone. At one end of the dam was the control works. And from this, a wooden pinstock ran down to the mill. This pinstock, as I recall, was square, made of four large planks, each about two feet wide, making the pinstock about two feet square. And held together by iron straps and iron bars. This pinstock led down to the ladder wheel underneath the mill. This was not the older type of overshot wheel or undershot wheel, but it was a turbine type, which probably at that time was quite modern. This turbine was inside of a steel case, which I remember as being about four feet in diameter and perhaps four feet tall. The pinstock could be opened and closed by a wheel up on the floor of the mill. And the ladder turbine or ladder wheel drove the main shaft, which in turn drove a leather belt, which I remember as being over a foot wide, perhaps fifteen inches wide. And this led up to the shaft of the saw. Of course, it also ran any other machinery that was in the mill, too. The saw was probably about four feet in diameter, so it was capable of handling rods up to two feet in diameter. The mill was normally operated by three men, a roller, a sawyer, and a marker. And if the fourth was available, why, he handled the slabs. The roller took the rods which were on the skidway and rolled them from there onto the carriage. Now, the carriage ran on a set of rails, similar to railroad rails, only smaller. And the carriage was probably around eighteen or twenty feet long altogether, and was made so that the upper part of the carriage could be moved crossways of the carriage toward the saw. The carriage ran back and forth right past the saw. So the roller rolled a log onto the carriage, and it was held there by two heavy iron dogs, which were sharp-pointed and raised up and down on steel posts. The roller would jam these two dogs into the log, and this would hold it. The sawyer would then move the carriage over by means of a handle, which he could pull on, move the carriage over so that as it passed by the saw, the saw would cut off a section of the log. And the first section that was cut off was called the slab, because this contained mostly bark. And usually the way they did it was they would saw the slab, in other words, take the first cut. Then the sawyer would run the carriage backwards, and then move the carriage over a certain amount. If he wanted a one-inch board, he would move it one inch. If he wanted a two-inch board, he would move it two inches, and so on. And then he would run the carriage forward, pass the saw again, and the saw would cut off whatever thickness of board the sawyer had moved the carriage for. After they'd taken off several boards this way, they would then roll the log over, so that the part which had already been sawed would be flat up against the carriage. And this time the two dogs were turned around so that a shorter dog would be holding the log in place on the carriage so that the saw could saw up fairly close to the end. Then they would continue the process of running the carriage forward, past the saw. The first cut would take off a slab, and then the next cut would take off boards, which had round edges. In other words, they still had the bark on the two sides of them. Now, if they wanted to have square-ed boards, they would merely cut four slabs off of the log, leaving a four-sided log, which had no bark left on it at all. And they would then proceed as before, and this would result in square-ed boards. Now, as the carriage went past the saw, the board, which had been sawed off from the log, would just fall over onto a bench. And then the marker would mark on it the number of board feet in that board. Of course, a board foot is a board one foot wide, one foot long, and one inch thick. So if you had a board which was ten feet long, one foot wide, and one inch thick, that board would have ten board feet in it. And the marker would add a piece of heavy crayon, and he would mark a ten on that board. If the board was the same size but two inches thick, it would have twenty board feet in it, and he'd mark a twenty on it, and so on. He also had a peg board hanging on the wall, and by moving the pegs into different holes in this board, he was able to keep track of the number of board feet of lumber that was sawed during the day by the mill. And on a good day, they would saw several thousand board feet of lumber. A keenster would then take this lumber, which had just been sawed, and load it onto his wagon, or, which was in the wintertime, his sled. And with a pair of horses, he would draw it from the mill out to the area where it was stuck up, so to speak. Sticking up this lumber consisted of first laying down some heavy pieces of slab on the ground, so as to get a fairly level foundation to start with, and also get it up off of the ground, because if the lumber was piled directly on the ground, it would rot very quickly. He would lay a couple of these heavy pieces of slab crossways, and then start laying the planks on top of them, and he would scrap with a pile perhaps five or six feet wide. When he got one layer of planks or boards laid down, he would then lay a couple of board sticks crossways of this layer of boards, and then put another layer on top. These board sticks were made by taking slabs in the mill and sawing them first into the top of length, five, six feet, whatever size they wanted for the lumber piles, and then splitting the slabs by means of a saw into pieces, which would then be five feet long and about one inch thick. They would vary in width, but this didn't make any difference, the core. And these board sticks were what separated the layers of boards or planks in the piles of lumber. And this allowed air to circulate through the piles of lumber and therefore cure or dry the wood in the lumber. And it was ordinarily cured for about a year before it was sold. Then, if it was sold, it might have been sold to the local box factory, the Swiss Triller Box Company in North Dana for making wooden boxes, in which case it would be drawn by a team of horses from the yard, so to speak, where the piles of lumber were down to the box shop. Or if it was sold to some outer town concern, my grandfather and my father sold quite a lot of lumber to the furniture factories over around Gardner and Templeton. If it was sold to one of them, it would be drawn down to North Dana and loaded into railroad box cars and then shipped by railroad over to the furniture factory. By the time I was big enough to see the mill, my grandfather had stopped operating it, and instead it was operated by Frank Doubleday. But I used to go over there as a kid quite often because it was only a quarter of a mile or so from my home there in North Dana. And particularly one period when my uncle, Glenn Page, worked there for Frank Doubleday, and Glenn was the roller. So I used to go over and watch him and he would instruct me in the proper techniques of rolling and so on and so forth, and I would also watch the operation of the mill. Another thing, too, I used to go over there quite often in the springtime, spearing fish.
